\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

% DRAFT for author review. Sources: Sections 6-10 and docs/REPORT_SKELETON_VALIDATION.md (A1-A15).
\paragraph{What history reads.}
The motivating account, that the rate at which the bearing turns reveals range, is not what the data show. The gain of the multi-antenna devices comes from the angle channels in every model family, but an unordered mean of the window reproduces it, elevation carries most of it, and it vanishes when the angles are exact. History is useful here because it lets an estimator average several noisy, coarsely quantized angle reports; since elevation encodes distance, the averaging appears as lower range error. This is a smaller claim than the one the study set out to test, and a more reliable one, because it was tested directly on seven model families. On the controlled grid the account is incomplete: sequence models keep gaining from longer windows where tree ensembles, even when given the window mean, do not, and shuffling the older reports, with the current one kept in place, removes most of the gain from long windows, so a part of the grid gain depends on the order of the reports. Whether that part exists at macro range, where one report carries much less, is not known.

\paragraph{How far the result extends.}
The gain is as large as it is because the simulated angle error is drawn independently for every report. With an error that persists across the window it falls from about $17\%$ to $6\%$ for the tree ensembles and $2$ to $3\%$ for the neural models. A real angle estimator's errors are neither fully independent nor fully persistent, and measured traces are needed to say where between the two it lies. Until then the report's headline should be read as the upper end of a range whose lower end is between a sixth and a third of it.

\paragraph{Devices without an angle.}
On uniform NLOS a single-antenna device is barely localized at all: its models are only $5$ to $6\%$ better than guessing the cell centre, with or without history. Signal strength from one base station, under about $10\,\text{dB}$ of shadowing, carries little positional information over a $96\,\text{m}$ cell. Under mixed propagation the tree ensembles gain about $1.2\,\text{m}$, and an oracle experiment links that to the propagation state, which a single-antenna snapshot cannot tell apart; the neural models share it only weakly. The earlier hypothesis that the benefit of RSS history shrinks with range was not supported.

\paragraph{The two regimes.}
The controlled grid and the macro cell differ in scale, in the information one RSS reading carries, and in whether the test users were seen in training. The grid result, that history helps without an angle, and the macro-cell result, that it does not, are consistent with each other but do not isolate the cause, and the macro-cell data did not support the one candidate cause that was tested directly. The $25\times25$ regression runs without an angle are mixed rather than negative and depend on the propagation: on line-of-sight data Random Forest degrades with history while XGBoost halves its error, to about the level of Random Forest's snapshot model; under uniform NLOS and mixed propagation both gain, XGBoost by $8.7$ and $9.4\,\text{m}$ and Random Forest by $2.7$ and $1.1\,\text{m}$, from no-history errors of $33.3$ and $29.9\,\text{m}$ (XGBoost), against $37\,\text{m}$ for always guessing the mean position.

\paragraph{Practical case.}
With history in place the choice of model matters far less than the input, and a boosted-tree ensemble that trains in seconds collects the full gain. The conditions that this report found not to matter, line-of-sight propagation, a blocked line of sight, and the two interfering base stations, are the ones a deployment is most likely to vary.
